This is the wrong location for an AI data centre.

A large-scale AI data centre has been proposed on irrigated farmland beside Travers Reservoir, in the heart of Little Bow country. We're the ranchers, families, and long-time residents of this land, and we're asking decision-makers to choose a different site.

Who we are

About the Committee

"The Little Bow AI Data Centre Committee is a collaborative, volunteer-led group formed to provide an informed and balanced review of the proposed large-scale AI data centre development in Vulcan County."

The Committee brings together individuals with extensive professional experience across a broad range of disciplines, including engineering, land and resource development, regulatory affairs, government, environmental and land-use matters, corporate and business leadership, communications and media, and law.

This diverse expertise allows the Committee to assess the proposal from multiple perspectives including land-use planning, infrastructure, environmental impacts, public safety, regulatory compliance, economic development, and procedural fairness.

Know the facts

The Proposal

Here's what has been filed, what it would mean for the land around Travers Reservoir and Little Bow, and where the process stands today. We update this page as new filings become public always with a source.

What's being proposed

The proposal is to build a large-scale AI data centre campus on irrigated agricultural land adjoining Travers Reservoir. A project of this kind requires a substantial, continuous electrical power supply and significant water for cooling, along with new supporting infrastructure.

The proponent's application states the project would bring investment and jobs to the region. Community members have asked for clearer evidence on water sourcing and allocation, power infrastructure requirements, the amount of productive farmland that would be taken out of production, and the long-term economic case questions we're tracking below and in more depth on the Issues page.

Community concerns raised so far

  • Loss of some of Alberta's most productive irrigated farmland to industrial development.
  • Water demand for cooling competing with an irrigation allocation that is already fully committed.
  • New high-capacity power infrastructure and generation needed to serve the facility.
  • Noise, light, and 24/7 operations next to a rural and recreational area.
  • Limited detail so far on independent water and environmental impact assessments.
  • Questions about long-term local economic benefit relative to the footprint and resources required.

The Issues

The questions we're asking, organized by topic. Each section links to the research, filings, and organizations behind it. We'll keep adding sources as the record grows.

Environmental & Water+

Cooling a facility of this scale requires a substantial, reliable water supply. We're asking what source is proposed, how it interacts with existing irrigation allocations that are already fully committed in this district, and what independent assessment exists of the impact on Travers Reservoir and the surrounding watershed.

  • Alberta Utilities Commission project filings
  • Local irrigation district water allocation background
Agricultural Land+

This site sits on productive, irrigated farmland a limited and difficult-to-replace resource in this part of Alberta. We're tracking how much land the project would take out of agricultural production, permanently, and what that means for the district's long-term food and farming capacity.

  • Provincial farmland loss data
Power & Energy+

A facility like this requires a large, continuous electrical supply. We're asking what new generation or transmission infrastructure would be needed, who pays for it, and how it fits within the province's broader grid planning.

  • Alberta Electric System Operator planning documents
Legal & Regulatory+

We're tracking the approvals this project needs provincial, municipal, and otherwise and where each one stands. This includes county land-use bylaws, utility and environmental approvals, and any opportunities for public and landowner input at each stage.

  • Our filings & submissions
Safety+

Questions here include construction and operational traffic on rural roads, emergency response capacity for an industrial facility of this scale, and fire and water-security planning in a farming and recreational community.

  • County emergency services planning
Our story

History of Little Bow

Long before it was a line on a proponent's map, Little Bow was and is a working landscape shaped by the river, the reservoir, and the families who settled and stayed.

The Little Bow River and the irrigation works around Travers Reservoir have anchored ranching, irrigated farming, and community life in this part of southern Alberta for well over a century. Homesteaders broke the prairie sod in the early 1900s; irrigation and reservoir works through the mid-20th century turned dry grassland into some of the region's most productive irrigated farm country, while creating a reservoir that families and visitors still use every summer.

What's held the community together isn't nostalgia it's continuity. Many of the quarter-sections in this district have been farmed and ranched by the same families for three or four generations. The community hall, the irrigation district, the grazing co-op: these are institutions people built and maintained themselves, and they're part of why this proposal has struck such a nerve.

Early 1900s

Homestead settlement

Families begin homesteading the Little Bow River valley, breaking prairie for the first time.

Mid-1900s

Reservoir & irrigation works

Dam and canal construction reshapes the valley, forming the reservoir and enabling irrigated agriculture.

1954

Provincial park established

Recreation lands along the reservoir are formally protected, drawing generations of campers and anglers.

Little Bow residents organize to ensure the next chapter of this landscape is written with them, not around them.

The decisions on this proposal are still being made which means there's still time to shape them. If you're a resident of Vulcan County, your voice carries particular weight. Here's what you can do

01

Submit a formal comment to the regulator

Written submissions to the Alberta Utilities Commission and Vulcan County become part of the official record and carry the most procedural weight. Comments are strongest when they're specific cite the facts, the concern, and what you're asking for.

Submit a comment See filing guidance
02

Send a letter to your MLA and county councillor

03
